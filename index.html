Artificial Intelligence in Public Transportation

How data science could support safer, more reliable NYC transit—from predictive maintenance to smarter planning with digital fare data.

Bikash Bhowmik · Fall 2026

This article adapts my DATA 607 Data Science in Context class presentation. It discusses opportunities for artificial intelligence (AI) in public transportation, using New York City's MTA as a practical setting. The examples describe potential applications, not claims that the MTA has implemented every system discussed.

What does AI do in transportation?

Transit agencies collect information from operations, maintenance activities, passenger services, and fare systems. Machine learning can identify recurring patterns in these records; predictive analytics can estimate risks or future demand; and some computer-vision systems can help analyze selected images or video. AI should inform professional decisions rather than replace human accountability.

A basic data science workflow
Collect→ Clean→ Model→ Flag→ Human review
Reliable records, consistent timestamps, testing, and ongoing evaluation are essential at every stage.

Five possible applications

Predictive maintenance

Identify equipment with an elevated risk of failure.

Service reliability

Study patterns linked to delays and disruptions.

Passenger safety

Support selected hazard-monitoring tasks.

Demand forecasting

Estimate ridership and crowding by place and time.

Customer service

Assist riders with routine questions.

Predictive maintenance: a subway-door example

Unexpected equipment failures can interrupt service and require expensive emergency repairs. Scheduled inspections are important, but may not always reveal a developing issue. Predictive maintenance offers an additional approach: analyzing historical and current equipment information to estimate which components may need attention.

Consider subway train doors. Each opening and closing cycle produces mechanical wear. Relevant data could include door-motor temperature, number of operating cycles, equipment fault codes, and maintenance history. A model trained on prior failures might flag a door motor showing unusually high temperatures and repeated fault codes.

Illustrative process: Gather equipment readings → detect unusual patterns → estimate failure risk → notify maintenance staff → inspect and decide whether to repair.

This does not mean a model can guarantee when a door will fail. Its role is to help maintenance teams prioritize inspections before a potential service disruption.

OMNY: digital fare payments and data

OMNY, short for One Metro New York, is the MTA's contactless fare payment system. Riders can tap a contactless bank card, mobile device, or OMNY card. Fare transactions generate electronic records that, when appropriately protected and aggregated, can support analysis of travel patterns.

Important distinction: OMNY is a digital payment system. Its existence does not, by itself, demonstrate that AI is used to process fares.

Potential AI uses of fare-related data

  • Anomaly detection: flag unusual transaction patterns for review.
  • Fraud analytics: help prioritize suspicious activity for investigation.
  • Ridership forecasting: estimate station-level demand by time period using aggregated data.
  • System monitoring: detect unexpected changes in transaction volumes.

These are illustrative possibilities, not confirmed OMNY features. Any implementation would need privacy safeguards, careful validation, and human oversight.

Benefits, risks, and responsible use

Potential benefits include earlier warnings, better allocation of maintenance resources, and improved planning. Risks include false alarms, missed failures, biased or incomplete data, cybersecurity threats, privacy concerns, and implementation costs. Models must be evaluated over time as equipment and travel patterns change.

The goal is not simply to add more AI. It is to turn reliable, appropriately protected information into useful decisions—while keeping transportation professionals accountable.

Looking ahead

Transit agencies could responsibly connect maintenance, service, weather, and aggregated fare data to improve forecasting and operational planning. Effective systems would combine model performance monitoring, sound data governance, and staff expertise.

Conclusion

AI offers opportunities to make public transportation more reliable and efficient, especially by helping teams recognize patterns and prioritize preventive action. OMNY illustrates how modern digital systems generate data that may support future analytics, but payment processing should not be confused with AI. Trustworthy data, privacy protection, and human judgment remain central.
